## Overview

Every control law you write in later labs ultimately resolves to a throttle command sent to an ESC. Before you can reason about hover, attitude control, or trajectory tracking, you need a quantitative model of what that command actually produces: how much thrust, how much reaction torque, at what electrical cost, and how quickly.

In this lab you will characterize a complete propulsor — BLDC motor, ESC, and propeller — on an instrumented dynamometer test stand. You will measure thrust, torque, shaft speed, bus voltage, and current across the throttle range, and from that data extract the five parameters that define the propulsor model used throughout this course:

| Parameter | Symbol | Describes |
|---|---|---|
| Thrust coefficient | $$c_T$$ | Thrust produced per unit shaft speed squared |
| Torque coefficient | $$c_M$$ | Reaction torque per unit shaft speed squared |
| Throttle gain | $$C_R$$ | Steady-state shaft speed per unit throttle command |
| Throttle offset | $$\omega_b$$ | Speed-axis intercept of the throttle-speed line |
| Motor time constant | $$T_m$$ | How quickly the rotor responds to a throttle step |

You will repeat this characterization across a test matrix of propeller diameters and supply voltages, compare your results against reference data sets, and — critically — assess where this model stops being valid. The final discussion question asks you to argue for a better control approach than commanding raw propeller RPM. That argument motivates the closed-loop flight controller you configure in [Lab 2]({% link docs/labs/lab2.md %}).

### 1.1 Thrust, Torque, and Shaft Speed

The aerodynamic forces generated by a rotating propeller follow from momentum theory. Under a **static** (hover) assumption — that is, with no freestream velocity through the rotor disk — thrust $$T_i$$ and reaction torque $$M_i$$ of propulsor $$i$$ are modeled as quadratic in shaft speed $$\omega_i$$:

$$T_i = c_T\,\omega_i^2 \tag{1}$$

$$M_i = c_M\,\omega_i^2 \tag{2}$$

{: .important }
> $$c_T$$ and $$c_M$$ are **propulsor coefficients**, not motor constants. They are set primarily by propeller geometry (diameter and pitch) and air density. Swapping an 8 in propeller for a 10 in propeller on the *same* motor changes both coefficients substantially — which is exactly what the test matrix in Part 10 is designed to expose.

The static assumption is important and you will be asked to critique it. Equations (1) and (2) describe a propeller in still air. A propeller climbing, descending, or translating sees a different inflow condition, and its thrust at a given $$\omega$$ differs accordingly.

### 1.2 Power and Efficiency

Mechanical (shaft) power is the product of reaction torque and shaft speed. Electrical power is the product of bus voltage and current:

$$P_\text{mech} = M\,\omega \tag{3}$$

$$P_\text{elec} = V\,I \tag{4}$$

$$\eta = \frac{P_\text{mech}}{P_\text{elec}} \tag{5}$$

{: .note }
> $$\eta$$ as defined in Equation (5) is the **electromechanical efficiency of the ESC and motor together**. It measures how much of the electrical power drawn from the supply is delivered to the propeller shaft. It says nothing about how efficiently the propeller converts that shaft power into thrust, and it is not a measure of how efficiently the vehicle hovers. For hover performance, the relevant figure of merit is the thrust-to-power ratio $$T/P_\text{elec}$$ (commonly quoted in g/W), which you may find more informative when you compare configurations in Part 10.

The dynamometer directly measures thrust, torque, shaft speed, bus voltage, and current. Mechanical power, electrical power, and efficiency are all derived quantities computed from Equations (3)–(5).

### 1.3 Throttle-to-Speed Relationship

Given a constant throttle command, the motor settles to a steady-state shaft speed. Over the usable throttle range this relationship is well approximated as affine:

$$\omega_\text{ss} = C_R\,\sigma + \omega_b \tag{6}$$

where $$\sigma$$ is the throttle command and $$C_R$$ and $$\omega_b$$ are constants determined experimentally.

{: .important }
> **The numerical values of $$C_R$$ and $$\omega_b$$ depend entirely on the units you choose for $$\sigma$$.** Quan Quan normalizes throttle to $$\sigma \in [0, 1]$$; the RCbenchmark software reports it as a pulse width in microseconds. These give completely different numbers for the same physical motor. State your units explicitly in your report, and use the same convention for every configuration in your test matrix so that the values are comparable.

**A note on throttle units.** Depending on the application, "throttle command" can carry a variety of units. In the RCbenchmark software it is expressed in microseconds ($$\mu\text{s}$$), which may seem strange at first. This is a legacy of the [Servo PWM standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servo_control) adopted by hobby electronics for driving motors, servos, and similar devices.

The test stand — and, later, your flight controller — commands motor speed by sending the ESC an electrical pulse whose width is varied, repeated at a fixed frame rate (classically every 20 ms, i.e. 50 Hz). The pulse itself lasts between 1000 $$\mu\text{s}$$ (1 ms) and 2000 $$\mu\text{s}$$ (2 ms). Setting the pulse width within this range is what ultimately sets motor speed, via the ESC's internal commutation logic. Wider pulse, higher commanded speed.

<a href="{{ '/assets/images/labs/ESC_PWM.png' | relative_url }}" class="image-link">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/labs/ESC_PWM.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Servo PWM signal showing a 20 ms frame period and a 1000-2000 microsecond pulse width driving an ESC" />
</a>

**Figure 2.** The Servo PWM standard. The ESC receives a pulse train at a fixed 20 ms frame period (50 Hz), and commanded motor speed is set by the *width* of each pulse, between 1000 and 2000 $$\mu\text{s}$$. That pulse width is the throttle command $$\sigma$$ in Equation (6).
{: .fs-3 .text-grey-dk-000 }

{: .note }
> A constant pulse width is a constant speed command. Three different signals can each be perfectly steady and still command three different speeds — what distinguishes them is pulse *width*, not pulse rate.

<a href="{{ '/assets/images/labs/ESC_PWM2.png' | relative_url }}" class="image-link">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/labs/ESC_PWM2.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Three PWM signals A, B and C with different pulse widths mapping to three different constant motor speeds" />
</a>

**Figure 3.** Three steady throttle commands (A, B, C) sharing the same 20 ms frame period. All three are perfectly constant signals; they differ only in pulse width, and each settles the motor to a different constant $$\omega_\text{ss}$$. Frame rate does not set speed — pulse width does.
{: .fs-3 .text-grey-dk-000 }

### 1.4 Throttle Response Dynamics

Equation (6) only describes steady state. If throttle changes over time, the rotor cannot follow instantaneously — it has rotational inertia, and the motor has finite torque. The resulting dynamics are well modeled as a first-order low-pass filter from commanded to actual speed:

$$\frac{\omega(s)}{\omega_\text{ss}(s)} = \frac{1}{T_m s + 1} \tag{7}$$

where $$\omega_\text{ss}$$ is the steady-state speed commanded by the throttle setting (from Equation 6), $$\omega$$ is the true instantaneous shaft speed, and $$T_m$$ is the motor time constant.

For a step command from an initial speed $$\omega_0$$ to a final speed $$\omega_\text{ss}$$, the time-domain solution is

$$\omega(t) = \omega_\text{ss} + (\omega_0 - \omega_\text{ss})\,e^{-t/T_m} \tag{7a}$$

so $$T_m$$ is the time required to traverse **63.2 %** ($$1 - e^{-1}$$) of the *change* in speed:

$$\omega(T_m) = \omega_0 + 0.632\,(\omega_\text{ss} - \omega_0) \tag{7b}$$

<a href="{{ '/assets/images/labs/Motor_Step_Response.png' | relative_url }}" class="image-link">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/labs/Motor_Step_Response.png' | relative_url }}" alt="First-order step response of motor speed showing the time constant at 63.2 percent of steady-state speed" />
</a>

**Figure 4.** Idealized first-order step response, Equation (7a). The time constant $$T_m$$ is the time taken to traverse 63.2 % of the change in speed. Note that this figure shows a step **from rest** ($$\omega_0 = 0$$), which is why the 63.2 % line is drawn at $$0.632\,\omega_\text{ss}$$. Your step tests will start from a nonzero idle speed, so use Equation (7b) rather than reading the value off directly.
{: .fs-3 .text-grey-dk-000 }

{: .warning }
> A common error is to read $$T_m$$ off the plot at the instant where $$\omega = 0.632\,\omega_\text{ss}$$. That shortcut is only correct if the step starts from rest ($$\omega_0 = 0$$). Since your motor will already be spinning at the idle or minimum-throttle speed when the step is applied, you must use the general form in Equation (7b) or you will systematically underestimate $$T_m$$.

## Part 5: Test Stand Assembly and RPM Sensor

{: .warning }
> Before running any experiment, **make sure the dynamometer is firmly secured to the workbench using C-clamps.** Mount the motor on the test stand but **do not** attach the propeller yet.

1. Clamp the stand to the bench. Confirm it does not shift under hand pressure.
2. Mount the motor to the stand. Leave the propeller off.
3. Connect the bench power supply to the dynamometer, observing polarity.

{: .warning }
> Set the supply's current limit at or below the test stand's continuous current rating (55 A — see Part 6). A supply capable of delivering more current than the stand can carry will not protect the instrument. Confirm the supply stays in constant-voltage mode during runs; if it drops into current limit, the bus voltage will sag and your throttle-speed data will be invalid.

### 5.1 Configure the RPM Sensor

Use whichever probe your stand is equipped with.

**Optical RPM probe.** Apply one or two small squares of reflective tape to the rotating assembly so the sensor can detect them. In the **Setup** tab, select the optical RPM probe and set the **number of reflective tapes** to match what you actually applied.

{: .warning }
> An incorrect tape count scales every RPM reading by an integer factor, and that error propagates into $$c_T$$, $$c_M$$, $$C_R$$, and $$\omega_b$$ — all of them wrong, all of them still looking plausible on a plot. Verify the count before you collect data.

**Electrical RPM probe.** Connect the RPM probe to one of the motor's phase connectors (it can be inserted into the bullet connector) and set the motor's **pole count** in the software. The pole count relates electrical frequency $$f_e$$ to shaft speed $$n$$ in RPM:

$$N_p = \frac{120\,f_e}{n} \tag{8}$$

If your motor's specifications list a maximum electrical frequency and a maximum RPM, Equation (8) gives the pole count directly. Otherwise, count the magnets bonded to the inside of the motor bell — that count *is* $$N_p$$. Outrunners in the 920 KV class commonly have 14 poles.

{: .sanity_check }
> With no propeller and no load, a BLDC motor spins at roughly $$\omega_\text{no-load} \approx K_V \times V$$ in RPM. For a 920 KV motor on a 12 V supply that is about 11,000 RPM at full throttle. If your reading is off by an integer factor (half, double, seven times), your tape count or pole count is wrong.

## Part 6: Configure Safety Cutoffs

The test stand enforces limits that cut power when a measured quantity exceeds a threshold. Configure these **before** your first powered run.

### 6.1 Measure Winding Resistance

Using the software's built-in **Winding Ohmmeter**, measure the resistance of the motor windings. Take **three measurements — one for each pair of phases** — and average them.

### 6.2 Estimate the Current Limit

Estimate the maximum current using the supply voltage and the measured winding resistance:

$$I_\text{max} \approx \frac{V_\text{supply}}{R_\text{winding}}$$

For example, a motor with an average winding resistance of 1 $$\Omega$$ operated at 12 V gives a maximum current of 12 V / 1 $$\Omega$$ = 12 A, and a maximum power of 12 V $$\times$$ 12 A = 144 W.

{: .note }
> This estimate is the **locked-rotor (stall) current** — the current that flows when the motor is not turning and generates no back-EMF. It is an upper bound on the current the motor can draw at that bus voltage, not a prediction of what it will draw in normal operation. Note also that the winding ohmmeter measures phase-to-phase resistance, which for a wye-wound motor is twice the per-phase resistance.
>
> These starting limits will likely be conservative and may trip during normal runs. That is intentional — begin conservative, and once you have taken data and understand the actual operating currents, raise the limits only as far as your data justifies.

### 6.3 Enter the Cutoffs

In the **Safety Cutoffs** tab, enter your computed values. The **System** column gives the absolute limits of the instrument; **never enter a value outside these bounds.**

| Safety Cutoff | Min | Max | System |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Voltage (V) | 0 | User Input | 0 / 50 |
| Continuous Current (A) | −0.1 | User Input | −55 / 55 |
| Burst Current (A) | −0.1 | User Input | −60 / 60 |
| Power (W) | 0 | User Input | 0 / 2750 |
| Thrust (kgf) | User Input | User Input | −5 / 5 |
| Torque (N·m) | User Input | User Input | −2.0 / 2.0 |
| Motor Rotation Speed (RPM) | 0 | User Input | 0 / 1000000 |
| Vibration (g) | 0 | User Input | 0 / 8 |

Set the remaining cutoffs as appropriate for your motor and propeller combination.

## Part 7: First Powered Test (No Propeller)

{: .warning }
> Wear safety goggles. Confirm no propeller is installed.

1. Open the **Manual Control** tab.
2. The **Motor PWM** slider is locked by default. Unlock it using the checkbox beneath the slider.
3. Slowly raise the throttle and confirm the motor spins and that the RPM reading tracks it sensibly.

## Part 9: Data Reduction and Parameter Extraction

Export the logged data and process it in an analysis tool of your choice (MATLAB, Python, or Excel). A starter plotting script is available on the course Google Drive: [plotting script](https://drive.google.com/file/d/12xlMbD1CXtKSP970jx28k-ihtW5ofBog/view?usp=drive_link). It is a starting point — modify it to suit your analysis.

### 9.1 Convert Units First

{: .important }
> Equations (1)–(3) are written in SI units with $$\omega$$ in **radians per second**. The RCbenchmark software logs shaft speed in RPM and, depending on configuration, thrust in kgf. Convert before fitting anything:
>
> $$\omega\,[\text{rad/s}] = n\,[\text{RPM}] \times \frac{2\pi}{60} \approx 0.10472\,n$$
>
> $$T\,[\text{N}] = T\,[\text{kgf}] \times 9.80665$$
>
> Fitting Equation (1) in RPM instead of rad/s changes $$c_T$$ by a factor of $$(2\pi/60)^2 \approx 0.011$$ — nearly two orders of magnitude. Check the units in your exported CSV header rather than assuming; they vary with software configuration.

### 9.2 Extract $$c_T$$ and $$c_M$$

Plot **thrust vs. shaft speed** and **torque vs. shaft speed**, and fit each to the form of Equations (1) and (2).

{: .note }
> Equations (1) and (2) are **one-parameter** models: a pure quadratic through the origin, not a general quadratic $$a\omega^2 + b\omega + c$$. Fit the single coefficient directly (in MATLAB, `polyfit` with a forced zero intercept, or a least-squares solve of $$T = c_T \omega^2$$; in Python, `numpy.polyfit` on $$\omega^2$$ with `deg=1` and no intercept, or `scipy.optimize.curve_fit`).
>
> If you do fit a general quadratic as a diagnostic, report the linear and constant terms and comment on them. A large nonzero intercept at $$\omega = 0$$ means either a residual tare offset in your load cells or a genuine breakdown of the static model — both are worth discussing.

{: .sanity_check }
> For propellers of this class, $$c_T$$ should come out on the order of $$10^{-6}$$ to $$10^{-5}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{s}^2/\text{rad}^2$$ and $$c_M$$ roughly two orders of magnitude smaller. Both must be positive. A well-behaved data set should fit with $$R^2 \gt 0.99$$; a substantially worse fit points to a measurement problem rather than a modeling one.

### 9.3 Extract $$C_R$$ and $$\omega_b$$

Plot **throttle command vs. steady-state shaft speed** and fit a straight line per Equation (6). The slope is $$C_R$$ and the intercept is $$\omega_b$$.

{: .note }
> Fit only over the range where the motor is actually spinning — that is, at or above the minimum PWM you recorded in Part 7. Including the dead band below that threshold, where $$\omega = 0$$ regardless of command, will bias both parameters.
>
> State the units of $$\sigma$$ alongside your fitted values. If you fit in microseconds, $$\omega_b$$ will be a large negative number, because it is the extrapolation of the line back to a pulse width of zero — a command that is physically meaningless. This is expected and is not an error; it is a direct consequence of the units, and it is the reason Quan Quan normalizes $$\sigma$$ to $$[0, 1]$$.

### 9.4 Extract $$T_m$$

Plot the **step response of shaft speed vs. time** for one commanded $$\omega_\text{ss}$$ and determine $$T_m$$ using Equation (7b).

{: .note }
> If the logged data does not contain enough points to read the 63.2 % crossing accurately, fit Equation (7a) to the transient by least squares and extract $$T_m$$ from the fitted curve. Report which method you used.

### 9.5 Compute Efficiency

Compute $$P_\text{mech}$$, $$P_\text{elec}$$, and $$\eta$$ per Equations (3)–(5), and plot **efficiency vs. shaft speed**. Efficiency is a dimensionless ratio and must lie strictly between 0 and 1.

{: .sanity_check }
> If any computed $$\eta$$ exceeds 1, you have a unit error — almost always RPM used where rad/s was required in Equation (3). Go back to Part 9.1.

## Troubleshooting Reference

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stand does not appear in the GUI | Serial port permissions (Linux) | `sudo usermod -aG dialout $USER`, then log out and back in |
| Stand does not appear in the GUI | Wrong software installed | Install **RCBenchmark**, not Flight Stand |
| Thrust or torque reads zero under load | Load cell not connected, or not tared | Reseat the connector; re-tare with no load applied |
| Readings drift continuously | Thermal drift, or tare taken under load | Let the stand settle, re-tare with no load |
| RPM off by an integer factor | Wrong reflective tape count, or wrong pole count | Recount the tape squares or the bell magnets; correct the value in the Setup tab |
| RPM reads zero while motor spins | Probe misaligned, or unreflective surface | Reposition the optical probe; verify tape is clean and in the sensor's line of sight |
| Cutoff trips during a normal run | Starting limits are conservative by design | Confirm the actual measured value is safe, then raise that specific limit within the System bounds |
| Bus voltage sags at high throttle | Supply has entered current limit | Raise the supply current limit (staying at or below 55 A) or reduce throttle range |
| $$\eta \gt 1$$ in computed results | RPM used in place of rad/s in Equation (3) | Apply the conversion in Part 9.1 |
| Step transient has too few points to fit | Logging interval too long | Shorten the logging interval and rerun the step test |
| Thrust vs. RPM fit has a large intercept | Residual load cell offset, or static model breakdown | Re-tare and repeat; if it persists, discuss it in your report |
